# Leave one person out (follow-up 3, exploratory)

Numbers: `results/loso/summary.md` (generated by `scripts/loso.py`).

The holdout reversed the development result: without calibration, the average-beat baseline beat the
U-Net for all 4 holdout people. In development, the U-Net had won for 14 of 16. One possible cause is
training-set size: the development folds trained on 10 people, the holdout models on 16. So each of the
16 development people was tested again, with models trained on the other 15. The U-Net used the holdout
rule (2 epochs, 3 seeds); the baseline was fitted and tuned on the same 15 people.

| | mean r, U-Net | mean r, baseline | U-Net better for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 folds (train on 10) | 0.62 | 0.37 | 14 / 16 |
| leave one out (train on 15) | 0.65 | 0.41 | 14 / 16 (p = 0.001) |
| holdout (train on 16, 4 new people) | 0.60 | 0.69 | 0 / 4 |

- **More training people help both methods a little:** the U-Net by +0.03 and the baseline by +0.04.
  The ranking does not change.
- **So the holdout reversal is about which people were tested, not how many were trained on.** The
  baseline's score depends heavily on whether its SCG beat detector works for a person and whether the
  population beat shape fits them. Across development people it ranges from 0.01 to 0.68. The four
  holdout people sit at the top of that range, where the U-Net sits as usual.
- This is exploratory. These 16 people have been looked at throughout development, so it explains the
  holdout result; it does not overturn it. The robust statements are the ones that held everywhere:
  - calibration works;
  - no GAN reliably helps;
  - without calibration, which of the two methods wins depends on the people tested.
