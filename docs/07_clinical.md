# Would a clinician accept it? (Phase 5 note)

Numbers: `results/clinical/summary.md` (generated by `scripts/clinical.py`). Unseen-person setting, 16
development people, test data = the 'late' part. Every signal (the real ECG and every reconstruction) is
delineated by the same method (neurokit2 'dwt') at the database's R-peak marks, and its intervals are
compared beat by beat with the real ECG's.

## Intervals: median per-beat error against the real ECG (ms)

| method | QRS | PR | QT |
|---|---|---|---|
| average-beat baseline | 50 | 61 | 74 |
| U-Net | 46 | 34 | 52 |
| pix2pix (adv 0.1) | 48 | 34 | 52 |
| CycleGAN, paired | 41 | 37 | 54 |
| U-Net, shifted using 30 s calibration | 41 | 35 | 54 |
| person's own average beat (30 s) | **8** | 27 | 15 |
| U-Net fine-tuned on 30 s | 12 | **15** | **15** |

For scale: a normal QRS lasts 80-100 ms, and QT changes of 10-20 ms are clinically meaningful.

- **Without calibration, no model gives usable intervals.** The QRS error is half a QRS. The GANs are no
  better than the U-Net here either (no difference for any interval; p > 0.2).
- **With 30 s of calibration, the errors fall to 8-15 ms.** That is in the range where the delineator's own
  disagreements start to matter. The person's own average beat gives the best QRS (8 ms against 12; better
  for 11 of 15 people, 3 ties). The fine-tuned U-Net gives the best PR (15 ms against 27). QT is a tie.
- Biases are small for the calibrated methods (within 5 ms), except PR for the own average beat (+17 ms).

## Irregular beats

Beats whose R-R interval differs from the local median by more than 20%: 160 test beats, most from
subjects 4, 7, 14 and 18. They are timing irregularities (CEBS annotates every beat as normal), not
abnormal beat shapes.

| method | beat r, regular beats | beat r, irregular beats |
|---|---|---|
| average-beat baseline | 0.48 | 0.30 |
| U-Net | 0.74 | 0.65 |
| person's own average beat (30 s) | 0.98 | 0.73 |
| U-Net fine-tuned on 30 s | 0.99 | **0.97** |

Every method still puts a beat at the irregular beats (95-99%). But an average beat pastes the same shape
everywhere, so it fails exactly at the beats that differ, and those are the ones a clinician cares about.
The fine-tuned U-Net follows the actual vibration beat by beat and keeps their shape (0.97). This is the
real argument for a learned model over a template, and it only shows on the beats that are not average.

## Flat-line test: inputs with no heartbeat

One minute per fold and seed of: the real SCG; the SCG cut into 50 ms blocks and shuffled; white noise;
zeros. The result is in `results/clinical/flatline.png`.

| method | output SD, real SCG | output SD, shuffled SCG | output SD, white noise | output SD, zeros |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U-Net | 0.88 | 0.88 | 0.85 | 0.20 |
| pix2pix (adv 0.1) | 0.91 | 0.88 | 0.85 | 0.20 |
| CycleGAN, paired | 0.93 | 0.96 | 1.00 | 0.23 |
| cGAN, 2 generators / 6 discriminators | 0.93 | 0.96 | 1.01 | 0.21 |

- **Every model draws a full-size ECG from noise.** From shuffled SCG or white noise, the output has
  QRS-like spikes and T-waves at random times (neurokit finds about 90 "beats" per minute), with the same
  amplitude as the real case.
- On all-zero input the output is nearly flat, so unlike the original model, these models can draw a
  flat line. But a detached or noisy sensor does not give zeros; it gives noise, and the output then looks
  like an ECG.
- In practice this means the output needs a signal-quality gate (is there a heartbeat in the SCG?) before
  anyone looks at it. None of these models, and neither paper, has one.
